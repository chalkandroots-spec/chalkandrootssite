Directions for Students

On this test, you will read/view THREE stimuli about the same topic. Real tests almost never give you just one source — they mix formats on purpose. Here’s how to handle each type:

  1. Print passage — Read it like an article. Underline or jot down any fact, statistic, or expert statement you might use as evidence.
  2. Graph/chart — Don’t skip this because it “isn’t words.” Read the title, the labels on each axis, and the numbers first. A graph is evidence too — one specific number from it is often stronger than a whole paragraph of opinion.
  3. Video / transcript — Whether you watch it or read the transcript, treat it the same as a print passage: find the speaker’s main claim and one specific detail they give to support it.

You do not have to use all three stimuli, but a strong response usually pulls evidence from at least TWO different ones.

Stimulus 1 — Print Passage

Stimulus 1 Print Passage

AI Tools Enter the Elementary Classroom

Across the country, elementary teachers are testing AI-powered reading and math programs that adjust each problem’s difficulty to the student answering it. Supporters say the technology gives teachers real-time data they used to have to gather by hand, freeing up time for small-group instruction.

Critics point out that not every school can afford the software or the training teachers need to use it well, and some worry students will lean on the tool instead of building their own reasoning skills. Districts that have piloted these programs report mixed early results: some schools saw reading scores rise within a semester, while others saw little change and pointed to inconsistent internet access as a likely cause.

Writing Prompt

Argumentative Essay

Should elementary schools use AI tools in the classroom?

Using evidence from at least TWO of the three stimuli above, write an argumentative essay that states a clear claim, supports it with reasons and evidence, addresses a counterclaim, and ends with a strong conclusion.

Scoring Rubric

1Claim & Argument Development

  • Clearly states a claim and maintains a precise argument throughout the essay.
  • Effectively addresses counterclaims or opposing viewpoints.
  • Demonstrates logical reasoning and insight in connecting evidence to the claim.

2Evidence & Elaboration

  • Uses relevant and sufficient evidence from provided stimuli to support claims.
  • Explains and elaborates on evidence, showing clear understanding of the topic.
  • Integrates evidence to strengthen the argument and refute counterclaims.

3Organization & Cohesion

  • Presents a cohesive structure with a clear introduction, body, and conclusion.
  • Uses effective transitions to clarify relationships among claims, reasons, and evidence.
  • Maintains a logical progression of ideas throughout the essay.

4Language & Style

  • Demonstrates command of precise language and domain-specific vocabulary.
  • Maintains a formal style and objective tone appropriate for argumentative writing.
  • Uses varied sentence structures and sophisticated transitions.

5Conventions

  • Exhibits correct grammar, punctuation, spelling, and mechanics.
  • Minor errors may be present but should not interfere with meaning.
  • High-scoring essays contain few or no errors.

Key components adapted from a Tennessee-style argumentative essay rubric.

High — Advanced/Proficient

Sophisticated argument, well-integrated evidence, clear reasoning, cohesive structure, precise language, minimal errors.

Mid — Basic/Developing

Adequate claim and evidence, some organizational or reasoning weaknesses, occasional errors in language or mechanics.

Low — Below Basic

Weak or unclear claim, insufficient or irrelevant evidence, poor organization, frequent errors that interfere with meaning.
